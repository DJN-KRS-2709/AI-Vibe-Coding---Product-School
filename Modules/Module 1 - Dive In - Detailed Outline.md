## Detailed Session Plan (2 Hours)

## Module Overview

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| **Duration** | 120 minutes |
| **Confidence Line Position** | Far left. Maximum ambiguity. |
| **Core Principle** | DO first, LEARN second. Build three times before naming a single framework. |
| **Tool** | Lovable (Pro accounts, no credit issues) |
| **Pre-requisite** | Pre-Work complete: Lovable account set up, basic page built, Confidence Line primer watched |
| **What students walk out with** | 2 prototypes (one raw, one leveled-up), a completed Build-Show-Learn-Decide cycle, and the Confidence Line framework named retroactively |

### The Energy Contract

Carlos said it plainly: "I want people complaining because this is too advanced." Module 1 sets that tone. Students who walk in have done some basic prompting before. We don't repeat what they already know. We show them what they *don't* know yet — and we do it by building, not by lecturing.

**The unspoken rule:** If the instructor is talking for more than 5 consecutive minutes without something being built on screen, something is wrong.

**Terminology (consistent across all Product School courses):**
- **Demo** = instructor building live
- **Hands-on lab** = students building in the tool
- **Breakout group activity** = students working in groups (e.g., swapping prototypes, filling templates)

#### 0:13 - 0:28 | Instructor Demo: "Three Directions in 15 Minutes" (15 min)

**Purpose:** Show students the power AND the sophistication of vibe coding. Not just "look how fast I can build" -- but "look how I can build three completely different things AND make one look like it already belongs in your company's product."

**Setup:** One deliberately vague, messy, real-world problem statement displayed on screen (Slide 5). The slide includes the problem quote plus a closing line:

> **The Problem:** "Our enterprise customers are churning 30 days after onboarding. The VP of Customer Success thinks it's a UX issue. The Head of Product thinks we need better feature discovery. The CEO thinks we should add a self-serve analytics dashboard. Nobody has data. Everybody has opinions."
>
> This is the reality of product work. Competing theories, zero validation, and pressure to ship something. Sound familiar? This is exactly the kind of mess vibe coding helps you navigate—fast.

**Instructor builds 3 prototypes live, one after another:**

**Prototype 1: "The Quick Exploration" (4 min)**
- Prompt approach: Single, open prompt. "Build me an enterprise customer onboarding dashboard that helps users discover key features in their first 30 days. Include a progress tracker, recommended actions, and a help section."
- What it shows: Speed. In 4 minutes, there's a clickable thing. It's generic, but it exists. Students see: "Okay, I could do that."
- Instructor narrates: "This is what most people do. One prompt, one output. It's fine. But watch what happens when we push further."

**Prototype 2: "The Reframe" (4 min)**
- Prompt approach: Different angle entirely. "Build me a churn early-warning system for enterprise accounts. Show a list of accounts with risk scores, last login dates, feature adoption percentages, and a 'reach out' action button. Make it feel like an internal operations tool, not a customer-facing product."
- What it shows: Same problem, fundamentally different solution direction. This isn't a variation -- it's a reframe. The first prototype assumed the customer needs help. This one assumes the CS team needs better tools.
- Instructor narrates: "Same problem. Completely different product. This is what 'divergent prototyping' looks like in practice. We're not iterating -- we're exploring."

**Prototype 3: "The Existing Product Clone" (7 min) ← THE WOW MOMENT**
- **Step 1 (2 min):** Open Mobbin. Find a real SaaS analytics dashboard (e.g., Stripe's dashboard, Notion's sidebar, Linear's project view). Screenshot the layout, the color scheme, the navigation pattern.
- **Step 2 (1 min):** Feed the screenshot into Lovable as context. Prompt: "Build me a customer health analytics dashboard that matches this design system. Use the same color palette, sidebar navigation style, and card layout. The dashboard should show: account health score, feature adoption by module, last 7 days activity trend, and a list of at-risk accounts."
- **Step 3 (4 min):** Build it live. The result looks like it *belongs* in the real product. It's not generic. It's not a basic e-commerce demo. It looks like Stripe. Or Notion. Or whatever template was used.
- Instructor narrates: "This is the part nobody teaches you. You don't start from a blank canvas. You feed the AI your company's look and feel -- a screenshot, a Figma export, a Mobbin template -- and you get a prototype that stakeholders recognize immediately. When you show this to your VP, they don't say 'that's a cool demo.' They say 'wait, is this already in our product?'"

**The punchline (after all 3):**

> "15 minutes. Three completely different products. One of them looks like it already ships at a real company. You're going to do the same thing in the next 20 minutes. But here's the difference: you're going to do it with YOUR problem. The one you posted in Slack. Let's go."

**Terminology:** *Demo* = instructor building. *Hands-on lab* = students building in the tool. *Breakout group activity* = group work on artifacts (templates, PRD, etc.). Use these terms consistently across the course.

**Common failure modes:**
- Demo takes too long: If any single prototype goes past its time, skip the polish and move on. The point is speed and divergence, not perfection.
- Lovable is slow/down: Have a 30-second video recording of the same demo as backup. Never waste class time on tool issues during the demo.
- Students ask questions during demo: "Hold that thought — build first, questions after." Don't break the momentum.

#### 0:33 - 0:53 | Hands-on Lab 1: "Your First Vibe Build" (20 min)

**Purpose:** Every student builds from an ambiguous brief. No methodology. No templates. Just: open the tool, read the problem, build something. This is the raw baseline. *Hands-on lab* = students building in the tool (vs. *demo* = instructor building).

**Hands-on Lab Brief (displayed on screen + posted in Slack):**

> **Your Brief (Slide 6):**
>
> Pick ONE of the four course scenarios below, or bring your own. These are the same scenarios you'll commit to in Module 2 and carry through to your final project in Module 6 — so pick something you'd want to keep building on.
>
> **Scenario 1 — The Retention Engine:** B2B SaaS losing 30% of customers in 90 days. Users "sign up, poke around, and ghost." Team invites matter. What's the real blocker?
>
> **Scenario 2 — The Internal Tool Nobody Uses:** CRM with 18% adoption. Reps say "too many clicks." CEO wants Gong. Can you fix adoption without replacing the tool?
>
> **Scenario 3 — The Marketplace Trust Problem:** Bookings flat. Zero-review providers can't get their first customer. How do you solve cold-start trust?
>
> **Scenario 4 — The Dashboard Nobody Reads:** Analytics with 60% bounce rate. Power users love it, new users flee. How do you serve both?
>
> **Scenario 5 — Bring Your Own:** A real problem from your company. Needs a clear user, a measurable outcome, and enough ambiguity to test.
>
> *Today is exploration — you're not locked in yet. But what you build here might become your final project. If you can't decide, pick Scenario 1 or 4.*
>
> **Two scenarios if you hit blank-page syndrome (Slide 7):**
>
> **Scenario 1 — "Copy and Customize":** Don't overthink it. Use one of these starter prompts. Replace the [brackets] with your specifics and hit go:
> - *"Build me a [community/social] feature for [fitness/sales/customer] users. Include a [matching screen / leaderboard / shared feed]."*
> - *"Build me a dashboard that shows [account health / sales pipeline / user activity]. Include a [filter / list / chart]."*
> - *"Build me a [type of app] that helps users [one action]. Include [one UI element]."*
>
> **Scenario 2 — "First Screen Method":** You have a problem but don't know how to prompt. Ask: *What's the first screen your user would see? What's on it?* Then: *"Build me a [that screen] for [that user] with [those key elements]."* Example: "Build me a sales rep dashboard with recent calls, CRM sync button, and quick-note field."
>
> **Rules (Slide 8):**
> 1. 20 minutes. Work alone. No collaboration yet. This is about your instincts and speed.
> 2. No methodology. No framework. Just open Lovable and go. Build on instinct.
> 3. Aim for clickable and showable. Not perfect. Not polished. Just something you can share via link.
> 4. Stuck? Prompt again. Still stuck? Prompt differently. Keep moving.
> **Produce:** A prototype you can share via link. That's the only success metric right now.

**What the instructor does during the lab:**
- Circulates (in person or via breakout rooms/chat). Checks on students silently.
- DMs anyone who didn't complete pre-work: "Hey, here's the quick setup link. You'll catch up in 5 minutes."
- Watches for students who freeze (staring at a blank prompt box). Approach them: "What's the first thing that comes to mind? Type that. Don't overthink it."
- Does NOT give methodology advice. If someone asks "should I frame the problem first?" say "Not yet. Just build. We'll name it later."
- At the 10-minute mark, announce: "10 minutes left. If you don't have something on screen yet, simplify your prompt. 'Build me a [one sentence]' is enough."
- At the 18-minute mark: "2 minutes. Whatever you have, share the link in Slack. It doesn't need to be finished."

**Expected outcomes:**
- 80% of students produce a clickable prototype (varying quality)
- 15% produce something very rough but functional
- 5% get stuck -- that's okay, the pair review helps them

#### 0:53 - 1:03 | Breakout Group Activity: "Show and Swap" (10 min)

**Purpose:** Students swap prototypes and test them as "users." This is the LEARN part of the cycle. It's also the networking moment — students meet someone, discuss product ideas, and build relationships.

**Group structure (Carlos/Dana feedback):** Don't overthink pairing. Since the final project is individual, it doesn't matter who is in which breakout room. Instructor: "How many people per breakout room? Press the button. That's it. Don't suffer." Random assignment is fine. Can use Donut or Zoom's random breakout. Groups of 2 for pair review, or 4–6 if they're in shared "rooms" where they build alone but can swap with others in the same room.

**How it works:**
- Put students in breakout rooms (pairs or small groups). In Zoom: use the breakout button — specify number per room, don't manually assign. In person: turn to your neighbor or use Donut.
- Each pair has 10 minutes total: 5 minutes per person.
- **Person A shares their link. Person B clicks through it as a user.** Person A watches silently -- don't explain, don't guide. Just observe.
- After 3 minutes of clicking, Person B gives candid feedback (2 min):
  - "What I understood immediately"
  - "What confused me"
  - "What assumption does this seem to test?"
- Switch roles. Repeat.

**Instructor script before the swap (Slide 9):**

> "Share your Lovable link with your partner. Here's the rule: you are NOT allowed to explain your prototype. Just share the link and watch them use it. Don't say 'click there' or 'that button does X.' Watch where they go, what confuses them, what they ignore. This mirrors real user behavior—they won't have you there to explain. You'll learn more from watching someone use your thing silently for 3 minutes than from any feedback form."

**What this teaches (without naming it):**
- Your prototype communicates (or doesn't) without you present
- Building reveals assumptions you didn't know you had
- Feedback from a "user" is different from feedback from a "reviewer"

**Format:** **Full-class discussion** — not breakout. Everyone back in the main room. Instructor moderates. Popcorn style.

You built something, put it in front of a real person, and learned whether your instincts were right. Most product teams spend months debating features in conference rooms. You just shipped, tested, and learned in the time it takes to have one meeting."

### Block 3: Level Up (1:03 - 1:40)

---

#### 1:03 - 1:13 | Instructor Demo 2: "From Basic to Pro" (10 min)

**Purpose:** This is the "things you don't know yet" moment. The instructor takes a basic prototype and upgrades it live, showing techniques that go beyond "just prompt and hope."

**How this differs from the first demo (Block 1):** The first demo showed *what* to build — three divergent directions from one problem, including the "existing product clone" wow moment. It was about *exploration* and *speed*. This demo is the opposite: we take *one* prototype and *improve* it. It's about *iteration*, not exploration. Block 1 = "here are three different things you could build." Block 3 = "here's how to make what you built look and feel like a real product."

**Starting point:** Prototype 1 from Demo 1 — the enterprise customer onboarding dashboard. It has a progress tracker, recommended actions, and a help section. It's functional but looks generic: standard AI-generated layout, default colors, no design system, no real interactivity. This is the "before."

**Upgrade Sequence (Slide 12):** Design System → Add Interactivity → Refine Prompts. The key is steering, not starting over.

---

##### Standardized Refinement Prompts — Use These Exactly

*All instructors use these prompts verbatim. This ensures consistent demos across cohorts and eliminates improvisation risk. Read the narration between prompts while the AI generates.*

**Demo 1 prompt (for reference — this is what produced the starting prototype):**

> Build me an enterprise customer onboarding dashboard that helps users discover key features in their first 30 days. Include a progress tracker, recommended actions, and a help section.

**That produced a working prototype in one prompt. Now we make it real.**

---

**STEP 1: Design System Matching (3 min)**

**Before prompting:** Open Mobbin in a second tab. Navigate to a pre-selected SaaS dashboard screenshot (Linear, Notion, or Stripe — have 2–3 bookmarked). Take a screenshot. Show it to the class: "This is Linear's project view. Watch what happens when I feed this into Lovable as context."

**Action:** Attach the Mobbin screenshot to the Lovable chat, then send:

**Prompt 1A — Full Design System Transformation:**

> Completely redesign this onboarding dashboard to match the design system in the attached screenshot. Transform every visual element:
>
> **Layout:** Add a dark sidebar on the left with icon + label navigation items. The main content area should be a clean white/light gray background with generous padding and clear visual hierarchy.
>
> **Cards and containers:** Rebuild every section — progress tracker, recommended actions, and help — as modern cards with rounded corners (8px), subtle borders (#e5e7eb), light drop shadows, and consistent 24px internal padding. Cards should have clear separation with white backgrounds that float above the page background.
>
> **Color palette:** Dark sidebar (#1e1e2d), light content area (#f9fafb), blue primary accent (#3b82f6) for buttons and active states, green (#22c55e) for completed/success states, gray (#6b7280) for secondary text.
>
> **Typography:** Use a clean sans-serif. Page title 24px bold. Section headings 18px semibold. Body text 14px regular. Secondary/helper text 13px in #9ca3af.
>
> **Progress tracker:** Redesign as a horizontal step indicator with connected dots/circles across the top of the main area — not a vertical list. Completed steps are filled green, current step pulses blue, upcoming steps are hollow gray. Add a progress bar line connecting the steps.
>
> **Recommended actions:** Display as a grid of 2x2 cards, each with an icon on the left, a title, a one-line description, and a subtle arrow or "Start" button on the right. Use hover-ready card styling.
>
> **Overall polish:** Add smooth spacing between sections (32px gaps). Make the whole page feel like a premium SaaS product — the kind of dashboard you'd see at Linear, Notion, or Stripe.
>
> Keep all existing content and functionality. Transform the design completely.

**While it generates, narrate:**

> "I didn't change what the dashboard does. Same progress tracker, same recommended actions, same help section. But I told it exactly how every element should look — layout, cards, colors, typography, spacing. I gave it a reference screenshot AND spelled out the details. That's the difference between 'make it prettier' and 'match this design system.' One gets you a random reskin. The other gets you something that looks like it belongs at a real company."

**After it renders — show before/after side by side (keep Demo 1 output in a separate tab):**

> "Look at these side by side. Same prototype. Same content. One prompt. The first version looks like a homework assignment. This one looks like it ships at Linear. That progress tracker went from a plain list to a horizontal step indicator. Those recommended actions went from a bulleted list to a card grid. Everything has consistent spacing, real typography, real colors. Your VP doesn't care about your prototype unless it looks like it could already be in your product. This takes 2 minutes and changes the entire conversation."

**If the output isn't perfect** (colors slightly off, spacing awkward): Don't fix it yet. Say: "It's 90% there. We'll fix specific details in Step 3. The point is: the conversation with your stakeholder just changed completely. This went from 'nice demo' to 'wait, is this already in our product?'"

---

**STEP 2: Adding Interactivity and State (4 min)**

**Narrate before prompting:**

> "Right now this looks good but it's a picture. The progress tracker doesn't actually track anything. The recommended actions don't respond when I click them. A stakeholder can look at this, but they can't experience it. Let's fix that."

**Prompt 2A — Interactive Progress Tracker:**

> Make the progress tracker fully interactive. It should show 5 onboarding steps: "Create Account" (completed), "Invite Team" (completed), "Set Up First Project" (current — in progress), "Connect Integrations" (locked), and "Launch First Workflow" (locked). Completed steps should show a green checkmark. The current step should be highlighted with a blue progress indicator. Locked steps should appear grayed out. When I click a completed step, it should expand to show a "Completed on [date]" note. When I click the current step, it should show a brief description and a "Continue" button.

**While it generates, narrate:**

> "One prompt. The progress tracker now works. Watch — I can click each step and see where the user is in their journey. Students who did the first lab — yours were static too. That's fine for exploration. But the moment you add interactivity, it goes from 'a thing I made' to 'a thing someone can test.' That's the difference."

**After it renders, click through the steps live. Show completed vs. current vs. locked.**

**Prompt 2B — Actionable Recommendation Cards:**

> Make the recommended actions section interactive. Each recommended action should be a card with: an icon, a title, a one-line description, and a "Start" button. When I hover over a card, it should lift slightly with a shadow (hover state). When I click "Start," the card should transition to show a brief inline explanation of how to complete the action, with a "Mark Complete" button. When I click "Mark Complete," the card should collapse, show a green checkmark, and move to a "Completed" section at the bottom. Include 4 recommended actions: "Invite 2 teammates," "Create your first project," "Connect Slack integration," and "Set up weekly digest."

**While it generates, narrate:**

> "This is where prototypes start feeling like products. A stakeholder can now click through the onboarding flow and say 'yes, this is the right sequence' or 'no, we should change the order.' They're not reacting to a screenshot — they're testing the experience. That's validation. That's a tool, not a toy."

**After it renders, click through 2 actions — hover, start, mark complete. Show the flow.**

**Prompt 2C — Loading State (quick polish):**

> Add a loading skeleton animation that shows for 1.5 seconds when the page first loads, before the dashboard content appears. Use gray placeholder blocks that match the layout of the progress tracker, action cards, and sidebar. This should feel like a real app loading data from a server.

**While it generates, narrate:**

> "This is a 15-second prompt. It adds perceived quality. When your stakeholder loads this and sees a skeleton animation before the content appears, their brain registers 'this is a real app.' It's a small thing. It matters."

**After it renders, refresh the page to show the skeleton loading. Pause for effect.**

> "Static → functional → polished. Three prompts. The prototype went from a screenshot to something your engineering team would look at and say 'okay, I see what you're proposing.' That's the goal."

**STEP 3: Multi-Prompt Refinement — Steering, Not Restarting (3 min)**

**Narrate before prompting:**

> "Now the most important skill. Something is off. The help section at the bottom takes up too much space and feels like an afterthought. And the header text is generic. Most people would start over. Or they'd re-prompt the whole page. Don't. Target the specific section. Keep everything else locked."

**Prompt 3A — Targeted Section Fix:**

> The help section at the bottom is too large and takes up too much vertical space. Replace it with a compact "Need Help?" bar that sits at the very bottom of the page — one line, with a search input on the left and two links on the right: "Documentation" and "Contact Support." Make it sticky so it's always visible. Don't change anything else on the page — keep the sidebar, progress tracker, and recommended actions exactly as they are.

**While it generates, narrate:**

> "Notice what I did. I told it exactly what to change AND what NOT to change. That's the skill — constraint injection. If you just say 'fix the help section,' it might rebuild the whole page. You steer by being specific about what stays and what goes."

**After it renders, point out that the progress tracker and actions are unchanged:**

> "Help section is compact and sticky. Everything else is untouched. One prompt. No rebuild."

**Prompt 3B — Copy and Tone Refinement:**

> Change the main dashboard heading from "Enterprise Customer Onboarding" to "Welcome back, Sarah — here's your setup progress." Change the subheading to "3 of 5 steps complete. You're ahead of most teams at this stage." Make the subheading a lighter gray (#9ca3af) and slightly smaller than the heading. Don't change anything else.

**Narrate:**

**Prompt 3C (optional, if time allows) — Micro-Interaction Polish:**

The animation should be brief (under 2 seconds) and not block interaction. **Narrate (if used):**

##### Common Failure Modes for Demo 2

- **Lovable rebuilds the whole page instead of targeting a section:** This actually teaches a valuable lesson. Say: "See what happened? I wasn't specific enough. It rebuilt everything. Let me add a constraint." Then re-send with the "don't change anything else" clause. Students need to see the recovery, not just the success.
- **The design system match is off** (wrong colors, spacing awkward): Don't try to make it perfect. Say: "Close enough to have the conversation. We'll fix specifics in Step 3." Moving forward is more valuable than perfection.
- **Progress tracker states don't work correctly:** This is common with interactive state. Say: "The logic isn't perfect yet. That's the kind of thing we'd refine in Module 3 with precision prompting. For now, the interaction model is what matters — the stakeholder can see the concept."
- **A prompt takes too long to generate (>30 sec):** Narrate while waiting. Talk about what you expected. If it exceeds 60 seconds, say: "Sometimes the tool thinks. This is real life — you learn to write your next prompt while the current one generates. Multitask."
- **Step 3 time is tight:** Prompt 3A (help section fix) is mandatory — it's the core "steering" lesson. Prompts 3B and 3C are optional polish. Cut from the bottom.

#### 1:13 - 1:28 | Hands-on Lab 2: "Level Up Your Build" (15 min)

**Purpose:** Students take their first prototype and apply the techniques they just watched. This is where the learning compounds -- they're not starting from scratch, they're improving what they already built.

**Lab Brief (displayed on screen):**

> **Level Up Your Prototype**
>
> Take your prototype from Lab 1 and make it better. Choose at least TWO of these upgrades:
>
> 1. **Design match:** Go to [Mobbin](https://mobbin.com), find a real product that has a similar layout to what you're building, screenshot it, and use it as context in your next prompt. Reprompt to match that design system.
> 2. **Add interactivity:** Make at least one element dynamic -- a filter that works, a detail view that expands, a form that validates. Static screenshots don't validate anything.
> 3. **Refine without rebuilding:** Identify the weakest section of your prototype and refine only that section. Practice the skill of steering, not starting over.
> 4. **Existing product track:** If you're building for your real company, feed in a screenshot of your actual product and ask the AI to match the look and feel. Then add your new feature on top.

**1. The Confidence Line: One Framework, Six Modules (5 min)** — Slide 15

Connect to their real work:

**2. Tool vs. Toy (3 min)** — Slide 16

> "Quick gut check. Tool: did it help you decide something? Did you learn if your assumption was right or wrong? Did it move you from uncertainty to clarity? Tools generate decisions. ## Instructor Decision Tree: Common Scenarios

### "A student didn't complete pre-work"
- **During Lab 1:** DM them the setup link. They can get Lovable running in 3-5 minutes. They'll miss the first few minutes of Lab 1 but should catch up. Do NOT stop the class.
- **If it's more than 2-3 students:** Something went wrong with pre-work communication. Announce: "If you haven't set up Lovable yet, here's the link. You have 5 minutes." But this should be rare.

### "Lovable is slow or has an outage"
- **Minor slowness:** "The tool is being slow. That happens. Keep prompting -- it'll catch up. This is real life."
- **Full outage:** Switch to the backup: "Open bolt.new in a new tab. Same process, different tool. This is why we teach the methodology, not the button." (This is the one time we mention an alternative tool.)

### "A student is frozen -- can't think of what to prompt"
- Walk over. Ask: "What's the problem you're trying to solve? Say it in one sentence." Whatever they say, type it as the prompt for them. "There. Now hit enter. You just started."

### "The demos run long"
- Cut Demo 1 to 2 prototypes instead of 3. The existing-product clone (Prototype 3) is non-negotiable -- it's the wow moment. Cut Prototype 1 (the basic one) if needed.

### "Pair reviews are quiet -- people aren't giving feedback"
- Give a specific prompt: "Tell your partner ONE thing that confused you. Not what you liked -- what confused you. That's the valuable feedback."

### "A student built something amazing and wants to show the class"
- GREAT. Use it. "Hey everyone, [name] just built something worth seeing. [Name], share your screen for 60 seconds." This creates energy and peer motivation. But cap it at 60 seconds.

### "Someone asks 'what framework should I use?'"
- "Not yet. Build first. We'll name the frameworks after you've lived them." Redirect to building.

## Appendix: Example Problem Briefs

*Module 1 is exploration. Students can try any brief without committing. Starting in Module 2, they pick one scenario (or their company problem) and carry it through M6. The full scenario library with module-by-module progression is in `Final Project - Requirements and Scenario Guide.md`.*

### Brief A: The Retention Engine (Course Scenario 1)
> A B2B SaaS company (project management tool, 5K paying teams) is losing 30% of customers in the first 90 days. The VP of Customer Success says: "Users sign up, poke around for a week, and ghost." The Head of Product thinks it's a feature discovery problem. The CEO thinks it's onboarding. ### Brief B: The Internal Tool Nobody Uses (Course Scenario 2)
> Your company's 200-person sales team has an internal CRM note-taking tool that was built last year. Adoption is 18%. Sales reps say it's "too many clicks" and keep using Google Docs. The VP of Sales wants "some kind of AI thing" to fix this. Engineering says the tool is "fine." Meanwhile, the CEO casually mentioned "maybe we should just buy Gong" in last week's all-hands.

### Brief C: Your Real Company (Your Product Track)
> Use the problem you posted in your Slack intro during pre-work. This is your actual work challenge. Build a prototype of the feature or improvement you think could help. Feed in a screenshot of your current product if you can — make it look like it belongs.

### Brief D: The Marketplace Trust Problem (Course Scenario 3)
> A peer-to-peer services marketplace (50K registered users, 8K monthly active) has a trust problem. Bookings are flat despite growing signups. ### Brief E: The Dashboard Nobody Reads (Course Scenario 4 — Existing Product)
> Your company's customer-facing analytics dashboard has a 60% bounce rate on first visit. 12 charts, 4 filter options, a data export button.
