## Slide 1 — Title + 3 Waypoints (0:00-0:03)

> "Title slide. 'The Validation Lens.' But we're doing something different here — we prime the three things right upfront. The instructor says: 'Three things today. Constraints, context, build.' These are the waypoints. We name them now and come back to them at the end.
>
> Then the bridge from M1: 'Module 1 was fireworks. Module 2 is aim.' And one clarification you specifically asked for — we tell students: 'This course operates in the solution space. We're not teaching you to find the right problem — that's product strategy. We're teaching you to test solutions faster.' That scopes us and prevents the 'I already know this' reaction from experienced PMs.
>
> 3 minutes. Quick and directional."

---

## Slide 2 — Instructor Demo: Never Start From Scratch (0:03-0:11)

> "Carlos, this is the big structural change. Instead of opening with methodology, we open with action. You said 'this will wake people up' — and you're right.
>
> The instructor does a live demo. First, a basic text prompt: 'Build me a retention dashboard.' Generic result. Placeholder text, default layout, looks like every other AI demo. Then, the same tool, same session — but this time with a screenshot of a real SaaS product pasted in, plus a structured prompt with specific metrics. The result looks like something someone actually uses at work. Real-looking data. The right design language. It matches the domain.
>
> Side by side. Same tool. The only difference is context. Students go 'wait, that's what this can do?' That's the hook.
>
> Then the instructor names the three entry points — Text-to-App for new concepts, Screenshot-to-App for existing products, Design-to-Code for Figma workflows. And you mentioned Figma's new code-to-design partnership — we give that a 30-second callout here.
>
> 8 minutes. This replaces the old 'Bridging Design and Build' slide that was buried on slide 13. Same content, moved to where it creates maximum impact."

## Slide 3 — Choose Your Scenario (0:11-0:18)

> "Right after the demo, while the energy is high — commit. Students pick their scenario. We moved this earlier because everything that follows (the methodology, the labs) requires them to have a scenario in mind. In the old version, the scenario came after 30 minutes of theory — by then students were zoned out.
>
> Four options: Retention Engine, Internal Tool, Marketplace Trust, Dashboard. Plus Bring Your Own for students with a real company problem.
>
> Per your note — the instructor explicitly says: 'This is a product exercise, not a startup pitch. You're building to test a hypothesis, not to launch a business.' That prevents someone from going off and trying to build their startup idea.
>
> Students post their choice in Slack. 7 minutes. No switching after today."

---

## Slides 4-8 — The Constraint Toolkit (0:18-0:30)

> "Carlos, this is where we addressed your biggest feedback. The old version spent 32 minutes on methodology — problem framework, assumption mapping, divergent prototypes, design systems, credits strategy. You said it felt like theory you already knew.
>
> The new version: 12 minutes total for ALL of it. Here's how:
>
> **Problem Framework** — 2 minutes. Instead of lecturing six elements in the abstract, the instructor shows one filled-out example. 'Here's what the Retention Engine scenario looks like when you frame it.' Students see it applied, not described. The only thing the instructor emphasizes: 'Element 6 — Explore. What are you learning? That's the difference between building a demo and building a decision tool.'
>
> **Risk Types + Kill Switch** — 2 minutes. Three categories: value, usability, feasibility. Each one maps to a fidelity level — value risk means a landing page, not a full app. Then the kill-switch question: 'Which assumption, if wrong, kills the whole thing? Build to test that one first.' The instructor asks students to type their risk type in chat — quick interaction, keeps them awake.
>
> **Validation Loop** — 2 minutes. Four steps: identify hypothesis, define constraints, build and stress-test, synthesize and pivot. The key insight is step 3 — you're building to see where it breaks, not where it works. The instructor drops a Validation Brief template in Slack. Students will fill it out in the lab.
>
> **Prompt Anatomy** — 3 minutes. Context, Reference, Constraint, Output. 'Think of this as configuring a builder tool, not chatting with an AI.' Quick and practical.
>
> **Context Data Packs** — 3 minutes. And Carlos, this is the new piece that makes M2 work. I'll explain."

---

## Context Data Packs — The Game-Changer

> "This is the thing that creates the wow moment.
>
> In M1, students opened Lovable with nothing. A blank page and a vague prompt. In M2, they open it with four inputs: their Validation Brief, a screenshot, a structured prompt — and a Context Data Pack.
>
> Here's what's in a data pack. For each scenario, students get three files. First — 8 to 12 user and stakeholder quotes. Real-sounding feedback from interviews, surveys, and support tickets. Stuff like 'I signed up because my manager told me to. I opened it once, couldn't figure out where my tasks were, and went back to Notion.' Second — a CSV with 15 to 20 rows of realistic quantitative data. Retention rates, adoption numbers, conversion funnels. Real numbers. Third — a full problem brief with the company context, competing stakeholder hypotheses, and constraints.
>
> When students build their prompt, they paste actual user quotes and reference actual metrics. The prototype shows real data and real user voice — not placeholder text. The result looks like a product a PM actually worked on for weeks.
>
> For students on the Bring Your Own track, we give them a blank template in the same format — they fill in their own data from their company. Same structure, their content.
>
> We've drafted all four data packs. They're ready for Dana to review."

---

## Lab 1: Frame + Gather Context (0:30-0:42)

> "First lab. 12 minutes. Students do NOT open Lovable yet — they're preparing the inputs.
>
> Three steps. Fill out the Validation Brief — 5 minutes. Download and read their Context Data Pack, highlighting the 2-3 quotes and 3-5 metrics that relate to their hypothesis — 4 minutes. Find a visual reference on Mobbin or screenshot their own product — 3 minutes. Post everything in Slack.
>
> The instructor circulates and watches for two things: students who skip the data pack and just write a generic brief — redirect them to the feedback file — and students who try to include everything from the data pack — redirect them to pick just the most relevant data points for their specific hypothesis.
>
> By the end, every student has a hypothesis, a visual reference, highlighted user quotes, and selected metrics. They're ready to build something dramatically different from M1."

---

## Lab 2: Build With Context — THE WOW (0:42-1:07)

> "Carlos, this is the centerpiece. 25 minutes.
>
> Students open Lovable and build their prompt with all four inputs. The instruction: 'Paste your scenario brief as context. Include 2-3 user quotes. Attach your screenshot as a reference. Add specific metrics with real numbers. Define exactly what screens you need and what hypothesis they test.'
>
> The instructor watches for three things. Students prompting generically without using the data — redirect them back to the pack. Students building something pretty that doesn't test their hypothesis — redirect them to their brief. And students who got a strong first result and are polishing aesthetics — redirect them to stress-test instead: 'Go to the edge case. What happens when the data is empty? When the user does something unexpected?'
>
> At 5 minutes remaining, everyone verifies their shareable link works. They're about to swap."

---

## Peer Review: Does This Test It? (1:07-1:17)

> "Same peer review structure as M1. Breakout pairs. 3 minutes of silent exploration — no context, no explanation, like a real user.
>
> But the discussion questions are sharper. Not 'is this cool?' but 'does this test the stated hypothesis?' 'Where does it break?' 'Tool vs. Toy — would this help the builder make a decision, or does it just look good?'
>
> The peer's fresh eyes surface what the builder can't see: whether the build actually serves the test."

---

## The Reveal: M1 vs M2 Side-by-Side (1:17-1:25)

> "Carlos, this is the wow moment you asked for. 8 minutes.
>
> The instructor asks 2-3 volunteers to pull up their M1 prototype and their M2 prototype side by side. Full class can see both.
>
> The M1 build: generic layout, placeholder text, default components. Built from a vague prompt with no context.
>
> The M2 build: domain-specific design, real user quotes in the copy, real metrics in the charts, structured around a specific hypothesis. Built from a Validation Brief, a screenshot, and a Context Data Pack.
>
> Same student. Same tool. Same amount of time. The only difference: constraints, context, and intent.
>
> The instructor asks the class: 'What changed? Why?' And then synthesizes: 'That's the validation lens. Better inputs produce dramatically better outputs. And the output isn't just prettier — it actually tests something.'
>
> This is the moment where students viscerally see the leap. That's the M2 magic — not a methodology lecture, not a framework slide. Their own work, made visibly better by the process we just taught them."

---

## Pulse Check + Quick Share (1:25-1:30)

> "Quick pulse. Students post a 1-5 production-readiness score in Slack with one sentence explaining why. The instructor reads a few — especially the extremes. 'What upgrade made the difference for you?' or 'What got in the way?'
>
> Then popcorn: 'One sentence. What assumption did you test? What did you learn?' 3-4 responses. The synthesis: 'The best outcomes aren't always it worked. Sometimes it's we learned this doesn't work — in 30 minutes instead of 3 months.'"

## Key Takeaways (1:35-1:40)

> "Mirrors the three waypoints from the opening. Full circle.
>
> One — constraint toolkit. Every build starts with a Validation Brief. Hypothesis, risk type, kill switch, success criteria. Not a blank prompt.
>
> Two — context injection. Screenshot, user feedback, real metrics — fed into the tool. You never start from a blank page again.
>
> Three — a prototype that's meaningfully better than M1. Pull up both. That leap came from better inputs, not more time.
>
> That's the validation lens. From now on, every prototype gets judged by what it validates — not how it looks."
