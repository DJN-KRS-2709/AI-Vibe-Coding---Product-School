# Module 2 — Carlos Walkthrough Script

**Purpose:** Slide-by-slide script to walk Carlos through the Module 2 deck. Use this as you advance through the slides.

**Flow:** Slide 1 (Title + Recap) → Ice Breaker → Problem Framing slides → Design Systems slides → Labs → Break It → Confidence Line → Wrap. 20 slides.

Two big things happen here: students commit to their final project scenario, and they learn to name what they're testing before they open any tool. I'll walk you through slide by slide."

**On screen:**
Two points: "Name It Before You Build It" + "Today You Commit"

**Say:**

Second: today you commit. Pick your scenario, carry it through Module 6. No more starting over — everything they build adds a layer to the same product. This is the accountability moment. Quick hit, then into the framework."

## Slide 3 — The Problem Frame (Reforge 6-Element)

**On screen:**
Six elements: Goal, Problem, Context, Constraints, Success Criteria, Explore

**Say:**

> "This is the methodology injection. Adapted from Reforge's problem framing — we use their 6-element frame but aimed at validation, not shipping. Goal, Problem, Context, Constraints, Success Criteria, Explore. The last one — Explore — is the key. That's 'what are we trying to learn by building this?' Students fill this out before they touch any tool. This directly addresses your feedback about depth: the depth comes from the methodology, not from tool instructions. We also built a one-page template for this — students paste it in Slack during the lab."

---

## Slide 4 — Assumption Mapping (0-to-1 vs. 1-to-N)

**On screen:**
For any product idea, identify the 3 riskiest assumptions. Which one kills the whole thing? Build to test that one first.
0-to-1: Risk is desirability. 1-to-N: Risk is adoption or integration.

**Say:**

> "This is the 'build to learn' discipline. Most teams build to look smart. We build to learn. Three riskiest assumptions — which one kills everything if it's wrong? Build to test that one first. And here's the important split for senior PMs: greenfield, the risk is usually 'do they want it?' Existing product, the risk shifts to 'will they find it?' or 'does it break what already works?' That's the 1-to-N lens you flagged as important — most of these people enhance existing products, they don't build from zero."

---

## Slide 5 — What Are You De-Risking?

**On screen:**
Three risk types: Value, Usability, Feasibility. Plus 0-to-1 vs. 1-to-N distinction at the bottom.

**Say:**

> "Carlos, this was pulled from the original course's 'What Are You De-Risking?' content — Dana reworked it. It gives students a concrete filter: is my risk about value, usability, or feasibility? Each one tells you where to focus your build. We added the greenfield vs. existing product split at the bottom. And it ends with a forward link to the lab — 'you'll choose your risk focus next.' This is where methodology meets action. About 3 minutes on this slide."

---

## Slide 6 — Divergent Prototypes & Fidelity Mapping

**On screen:**
Build 3-5 fundamentally different directions. Match fidelity to the question: Landing page → "Will they sign up?" / Clickable mockup → "Does this flow make sense?" / Functional app → "Can they complete the task?"

**Say:**

> "This is new — nobody in the original course taught this. Divergent prototypes means fundamentally different solution directions, not the same thing with different button colors. And fidelity mapping: don't build a full app when a landing page answers the question. This is where seniors separate from juniors — knowing the right level of investment for the question you're answering. Quick slide, powerful concept."

---

## Slide 7 — What Makes an Excellent Prompt (Prompt Anatomy)

**On screen:**
Four elements: Context, Reference, Constraint, Output

**Say:**

> "Carlos, remember your question — 'what makes an excellent prompt?' This is the answer. Four elements. Context: what's the product, who's it for. Reference: a screenshot or template so the AI knows the look and feel. Constraint: what must be there, what must not. Output: one screen or full flow? This replaces the old FUDI framework with something simpler and more actionable. FUDI goes deeper in Module 3 — here we give them just enough to build with intent in the lab."

---

## Slide 8 — Templates & Design System Sources

**On screen:**
Mobbin (free), Figma, Your Existing Product — "screenshot it, feed it in, match this look and feel"

**Say:**

> "This directly answers your feedback: 'How are you making sure the look and feel is as similar as possible to your current product?' Three sources. Mobbin — free, real product screens, use as reference. Figma — if their design system lives there, import it, we go deeper in M3. And the key one: screenshot your actual product, feed it in, say 'match this, add my feature.' That's the existing product lens. Module 3 goes deep on this with MCP and Figma integrations — here we plant the seed."

---

## Slide 9 — Credits Strategy

**On screen:**
Three steps: Plan in Gemini/ChatGPT → Paste into Lovable → Fix in code when possible

**Say:**

> "Practical slide. Dana flagged this: someone will run out of credits if we don't cover it. Three-step strategy. Plan your prompt in Gemini or ChatGPT — zero credits burned. Paste into Lovable when it's ready — one generation, one credit. And if it's a typo or wrong color, fix in code instead of re-prompting. This alone saves them 3-5x on credits. We mention it in M1 too, but here it gets its own slide because the labs are longer."

**On screen:**

## Slide 11 — Lab Part 1: Frame Before You Build (10 min)

**On screen:**
Commitment moment. Choose your scenario. Four course scenarios + Your Company Problem. Before you open Lovable, write: riskiest assumption, what you'll build, what fidelity, what validated/invalidated looks like.

**Say:**

> "Carlos, this is the commitment moment. They pick their scenario — carries through M6. Four course options plus their own company problem. But here's the discipline that didn't exist in M1: before they touch any tool, they write. Riskiest assumption. What they'll build to test it. What fidelity. What validated vs. invalidated looks like. Post it in Slack. That's their contract. The instructor circulates and checks that they're writing, not building. If someone can't name the assumption, they're not ready to build."

---

## Slide 12 — Lab Part 2: Build to Test (25 min)

**On screen:**
Now build. 25 minutes. Use frame as North Star. Apply prompt anatomy. Use Mobbin or screenshots. Build 1-3 divergent directions. Share in Slack with assumption.

**Say:**

> "Now they build — but with intent. Everything traces back to the assumption they named. Apply the prompt anatomy from the teaching block. Use design references. Build 1-3 divergent directions — different approaches, not variations. 25 minutes individual build. Everyone builds alone, but they're in breakout rooms so they can see each other working — the gym metaphor. Instructor circulates: 'What assumption are you testing? Does this prototype test it?'"

---

## Slide 13 — Breakout: Does This Actually Test It? (10 min)

**On screen:**
Peer validation. Person A shares frame + links. Person B reviews: Does this actually test the stated assumption?

**Say:**

> "This is the key differentiator from M1. In M1, the feedback was 'what confused me.' In M2, it's sharper: 'does this prototype actually test what you said you were testing?' That's the validation lens. The partner isn't evaluating if it looks good — they're evaluating if it tests the claim. If someone built something impressive that proves nothing, their partner says so. That's the valuable feedback."

---

## Slide 14 — What Did You Validate? (Full-class discussion)

**On screen:**
One sentence: What assumption did you test, what did you learn? Three buckets: validated, invalidated, still unclear.

**Say:**

> "Full-class discussion. Popcorn — one sentence each. Three buckets: validated, invalidated, or still unclear. The key synthesis: the best outcomes aren't always 'it worked.' Sometimes the best outcome is 'we learned it doesn't work — in 30 minutes, not 3 months.' That reframe is important for seniors who equate success with shipping. Sometimes killing an idea fast IS the success."

---

## Slide 15 — Chapter 4: Fidelity vs. Intent

**On screen:**
Chapter divider. Time to challenge the assumption that polish = value.

**Say:**

> "Transition to the Break It exercise. Every module has one."

## Slide 16 — The "Break It" Exercise (Toy vs. Tool)

**On screen:**
Two prototypes side by side. Prototype A (The Toy): high-fidelity, polished, validates nothing. Prototype B (The Tool): low-fidelity, rough, definitively answers the question.

**Say:**

> "Carlos, this is the Toy vs. Tool concept from Reforge's Ravi Mehta, applied to our context. We show two prototypes side by side. A: looks production-ready, stakeholders ooh and aah, but what did we learn? Nothing. B: ugly, one screen, but it definitively answers 'do users understand this flow?' The punchline: fidelity without intent is vanity. Clarity of question beats quality of build. Every time. This is the counterbalance to M1's speed — yes you can build fast, but speed aimed at nothing is waste."

---

## Slide 17 — Reflection Moment

**On screen:**
Take 60 seconds. Look at your own prototype. Does it test your assumption? Or did you build something impressive that doesn't answer the question?

**Say:**

> "Sixty seconds of honest self-assessment. No wrong answers. This is the hardest skill in product management — being willing to admit that the thing you built might be the wrong thing, even when it looks good. We don't have this in the old course. It's a deliberate pause."

---

Frame Assumption → Build Prototype → Test & Measure → Decide Outcome. Continuous cycle.

**Say:**

The visual was adapted from the old course's 'Rapid Validation Loop' — same concept, updated labels. This loop is their operating system for the rest of the course and for every product decision after."
