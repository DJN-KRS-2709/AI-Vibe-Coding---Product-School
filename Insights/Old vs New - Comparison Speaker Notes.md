# AI Prototyping vs. Vibe Coding — Comparison Deck Speaker Notes

## Slide 1 | Title — AI Prototyping → Vibe Coding

- Open with: "This isn't a content refresh. This is a fundamentally different course."
- Three headline numbers tell the story:
  - **6 modules redesigned** — every single one, not a partial update
  - **55%+ hands-on ratio** — up from 30–40% in the old course
  - **6 tangible deliverables** — students leave with assets, not just a certificate
- Frame this as a response to two cohorts of feedback AND Carlos's direct vision from the Feb 13 working session
- Don't linger — this is the setup, the evidence is coming

---

## Slide 2 | The Shift — One-Line Summary

- Let the two lines sit. The struck-through old line speaks for itself.
- **Old:** "A Lovable tutorial disguised as a PM certification." This was the single most common complaint across both cohorts. Students literally said "it felt like a long Lovable tutorial."
- **New:** "A product methodology that happens to use Lovable — students ship real, deployed, data-driven products." The tool supports the framework; the framework doesn't depend on the tool.
- The Carlos quote at the bottom is from the Feb 13 working session. He was emphatic: "This is the real deal where you actually can ship product code to production."
- Key message: we killed the tutorial. We built a methodology.

---

## Slide 3 | Course Identity — Side-by-Side Table

Walk the table row by row:

- **Positioning:** Old said "learn to prototype." New says "build, ship, iterate." Prototyping is a step in the process, not the destination.
- **Audience:** We stopped assuming beginners. These are senior PMs — they've built products. They need a repeatable methodology, not a tool walkthrough.
- **Deliverables:** This is the biggest gap. Old: group prototype + certificate. New: 6 real assets — deployed product, Living PRD, GitHub repo, Engineering Handoff Note, Living Prompt Pack, data-driven presentation. They can use these at work tomorrow.
- **Tool dependency:** When Lovable ships a new model or changes UI, old course breaks. New course doesn't — because we teach the method, not the buttons.
- **Arc:** Old course was flat — you could almost teach the modules in any order. New course has the Confidence Line — a deliberate progression from uncertainty to mastery.

## Slide 4 | Module 1 — From theory-first to build-first

- **Old problem:** First 30+ minutes were slides about why AI is useful. Senior PMs already know. Energy dropped before anyone touched a tool. Instructor demos weren't exciting enough.
- **New approach:** Zero theory upfront. Instructor demos a full build in 5 minutes. Every student builds their own working prototype within the first 15 minutes.
- **The numbers:** Hands-on went from ~30% to ~55%. Every student builds individually — no more one-person-builds-while-group-watches.
- Carlos said in the Feb 17 session: "You are going to be able to build your own applications end to end. Boom. That is fantastic. I don't think there is much more to add at this point."
- **Verdict:** Old M1 assumed students didn't know AI existed. New M1 assumes they do and gets them building immediately. Speed confidence on day one.

---

## Slide 5 | Module 2 — From prompt tricks to product validation

- **Old problem:** Feedback was brutal: "too basic for an advanced course." Teaching system prompts and token windows to senior PMs. The guessing exercise felt low-value. 20-minute lab was too short.
- **New approach:** Reframed entirely around validation. Not "write better prompts" but "build the right thing." Introduced Context Data Packs — real feedback, real metrics, real scenario briefs — so students aren't building in a vacuum.
- **The shift:** Students now build with data, form a hypothesis, and output a Validation Brief alongside their prototype.
- **Verdict:** Old M2 taught prompting as a standalone skill. New M2 teaches prompting as a means to validate product assumptions — the actual PM skill. This is the difference between a tool tutorial and a methodology.

---

## Slide 6 | Module 3 — From document exercises to building exercises

- **Old problem:** Terminology confusion between "Simplified PRD" and "PRO." First hour was overwhelming — links, docs, templates dumped all at once. Feedback called it "messy."
- **New approach:** Clean 3-prompt chain pattern (foundation → enhance → polish). Students build multi-screen prototypes with interactive states. The Living Prompt Pack — the reusable artifact — emerges naturally from the work.
- **The key shift:** Old M3 said "write documents, then build." New M3 says "build precisely, and the documentation writes itself." That's a much more authentic PM workflow.
- **Verdict:** Old M3 was a document exercise. New M3 is a building exercise where the documentation emerges from the work, not the other way around.

---

## Slide 7 | Module 4 — From debugging a tool to owning real infrastructure

- **Old problem:** The most damning feedback: "An hour into the class, students still hadn't started building." Instructor demoed debugging while students watched. Chat vs Agent mode was confusing.
- **New approach:** The infrastructure moment. Students connect their prototype to real systems: GitHub for version control, Supabase for database. AI extracts a Living PRD. They start an Engineering Handoff Note.
- **Carlos's distinction made concrete:** This is where "playing designer vs. building for real" stops being a quote and becomes an experience. Students now own real assets — a GitHub repo, a database project — not just a file in someone else's tool.
- **Verdict:** Old M4 taught debugging inside a tool. New M4 teaches the infrastructure transition. Students own real assets.

---

## Slide 8 | Module 5 — From "connected" to "deployed"

- **This is the slide to pause on.** Carlos said exactly this in the Feb 13 session: "I can connect to a database. I can import from Cloud. I can connect to Stripe. Oh, shit. I can push something to production. I think that would be magic."
- **Old M5:** Stopped at "connected to an API." Students couldn't connect "integration" to "this works in production."
- **New M5:** Delivers all of it — real database schemas, real authentication, real error handling — AND deployment to a live URL. Students pull out their phones and load their own product. That's the wow moment.
- **Why deployment moved here:** In the old course, deployment was in M6 and was anti-climactic — clicking one button after an hour of filler. In the new course, deployment is the natural climax of "make it real." This freed M6 to become something much more valuable.
- **Verdict:** Old M5 stopped at "connected." New M5 goes all the way to "deployed." Carlos's exact quote, realized.

---

## Slide 9 | Module 6 — From ceremony to closing the product lifecycle loop

- **The biggest redesign in the course.**
- **Old M6:** Deployment was supposed to be the finale but felt anti-climactic. One click. The rest was filler: polish checklist, presentations. Students knew it was padding.
- **New M6:** The most advanced module. Three stages:
  - **Measure** — connect analytics (Lovable Insights + GA4), see real usage data
  - **Learn** — feed metrics + problem statement to AI, get a coherent product analysis in 2 minutes
  - **Iterate** — build one evidence-based improvement, redeploy
- **The "aha":** Students paste analytics data and their problem statement into ChatGPT/Gemini, and AI produces an actionable analysis in 2 minutes. Something that would take a product team a day. That's a skill they'll use at work immediately.
- **Verdict:** Old M6 was a ceremony. New M6 closes the product lifecycle loop. This is what separates PMs from builders — the ability to learn from what you shipped.

## Slide 10 | Structural Changes — Beyond Content

Walk the rows quickly, but pause on three:

- **Build format:** The #1 hands-on problem. In groups of 4–5, only one person could actually use Lovable. Everyone else watched. Now every student builds in their own project, every session. Carlos's gym analogy: "You should feel the pressure of showing these people something you built."
- **Progression:** Old modules were disconnected — each one taught a different tool feature. New modules stack. By M6, students have a single product that evolved across all 6 sessions. The six-tab reveal makes it undeniable.
- **Instructor-proofing:** Two cohorts had instructor problems — one skipped labs, another didn't follow the revamp. New course has pre-built demo prototypes, detailed speaker notes, and Carlos Briefs. The structure forces hands-on even if the instructor is weak.
- Other rows (tool philosophy, hands-on ratio, wow moments) reinforce the same theme: systematic improvement, not cosmetic changes.

---

## Slide 11 | The Confidence Line

- **The old course had no narrative thread.** Modules were disconnected features of a tool. You could almost teach them in any order.
- **The new course has a deliberate arc:**
  - M1: Speed — "I can build something in 10 minutes"
  - M2: Validation — "I can test the right thing"
  - M3: Precision — "I can build exactly what I describe"
  - M4: Structure — "I can structure it for a real team"
  - M5: Ship — "I can make it real and ship it"
  - M6: Product — "I can learn from it and make it better"
- **The six-tab reveal:** Open all six browser tabs side by side — M1 through M6. See one product evolve from a rough prototype to a deployed, analytics-instrumented, evidence-improved product. The progression speaks for itself. No slides needed to explain it.
- By M6, students have gone from "I wonder if AI can build things" to "I just shipped, measured, and improved a product."
