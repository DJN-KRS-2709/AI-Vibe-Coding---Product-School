# Speaker Notes: Vibe Coding Certification Pitch

Casual talking points for each slide. Not a script -- just the key things to say and the energy to bring.

---

## Slide 1 -- Title

**Vibe Coding Certification: From ambiguity to deployed product in 6 modules.**

> "So here's what we've been working on. We're renaming and redesigning the AI Prototyping course from the ground up. The new name is Vibe Coding Certification. And the core promise is simple: students start not knowing what to build, and by the end they've deployed a real product backed by evidence. Six modules. Let me show you why we're doing this and how."

- Keep it short. Don't over-explain. The slides will do the work.
- This is the "lean in" moment -- set the tone that this is a real proposal, not a brainstorm.

---

## Slide 2 -- Why the Current Course Falls Short

> "Let's be honest about where we are. Three cohorts of feedback are telling us the same three things."

> "First -- it feels like a Lovable tutorial. Students want a methodology they can apply regardless of what tool they use. If Lovable changes its UI tomorrow -- and it does, every few weeks -- the content breaks."

> "Second -- it's too basic. These are senior PMs. They don't need 'what is AI' slides. They need frameworks for making real product decisions."

> "Third -- only one person gets to build. In the group labs, one person prototypes and everyone else watches. We already solved this in the Agents course. We need to do the same here."

> "The bottom line: NPS is at 3.8. The methodology underneath has good bones. But the way it's packaged isn't working."

- If Carlos pushes back ("it's not that bad"), point to the 3.8 NPS. That number speaks for itself.
- If he asks about specific feedback, you have the full survey data in the Insights folder.

---

## Slide 3 -- What Students Actually Said

> "These are real quotes from the end-of-course surveys. I pulled two critical ones and two positive ones on purpose."

> "The critical ones tell us: students want to build their own stuff, not watch demos. And they want frameworks, not templates."

> "The positive ones tell us: when they DO get to build, it's the highlight of the course. The hands-on moments are what stick."

> "So the signal is clear -- students love building. They want more of it, with better framing around it."

- Let the quotes sit for a moment. Don't rush past this slide.
- If Carlos or Fernan say "we've heard this before" -- that's your opening: "Right, and we've tried to fix it twice. But we were fixing the wrong thing. We kept going deeper on the tool. We need to go deeper on the methodology."

---

## Slide 4 -- The Confidence Line

> "So here's the new framework. We call it the Confidence Line. One narrative arc that runs through all six modules."

> "On the left, you start in ambiguity. You don't know what to build. Modules 1 and 2 live here -- you build fast, you test assumptions, you figure out what's worth pursuing."

> "In the middle, you gain clarity. Modules 3 and 4 -- you get precise with your prompting, you recognize when it's time to stop exploring and start committing. We call that the graduation moment."

> "On the right, you're confident. Modules 5 and 6 -- you add real integrations, handle edge cases, deploy to a live URL, and present to stakeholders."

> "Every student moves from left to right. The tool doesn't matter. The progression does. That's what makes this durable -- even if every AI tool changes next month, this framework stays the same."

- This is the most important slide. Slow down here.
- If Carlos is visual, point to the gradient: "Red is ambiguity. Green is confidence. The whole course is about moving students from red to green."
- This is also where you preempt the "but what about Lovable?" question: the framework is the durable layer, not the tool.

---

## Slide 5 -- Pre-Work + 6 Modules

> "Quick overview of the structure. We added a pre-work module -- async, about 30 minutes. Students set up their tools and do their first build before Day 1. This solves the problem we had in every cohort where the first 30 minutes of M1 was lost to account setup and subscription issues."

> "Then six live modules. I'll walk you through the highlights:"

> "M1 -- you build your first prototype in 15 minutes. No lectures. No preamble. You're building from minute one."

> "M2 -- now that you can build fast, learn to build smart. Name the assumption before you open the tool."

> "M3 -- precision prompting. Stop saying 'build me something' and start saying 'build me exactly this.'"

> "M4 -- the graduation moment. This is our biggest differentiator. When do you stop vibing and start structuring? Nobody else teaches this."

> "M5 -- make it real. APIs, edge cases, and how to hand this off to engineering."

> "M6 -- ship it. Deploy, present, compete. Full group build day."

- Don't read every module. Hit M1 (the hook -- 15 minutes), M4 (the differentiator), and M6 (the payoff). The rest you can breeze through.

---

## Slide 6 -- This Isn't a Light Rework

> "Now I want to be transparent about the scope of this. Dana and I mapped every old module to the new one. This isn't a slide refresh."

> "Three modules are rebuilt from scratch -- M1, M2, and M3. The content is fundamentally different. New themes, new labs, new learning objectives."

> "Three more are restructured -- M4, M5, M6. Same general territory but reorganized, tightened, and upgraded."

> "And we added one entirely new module -- the pre-work."

> "On top of that, we've built a new instructor guide with minute-by-minute timing for every module, an assessment rubric, and success metrics. This is a significant lift."

- This is Dana's slide. The point is to set realistic expectations on timeline.
- If Carlos says "can we have it ready in two weeks?" -- point to the red tags: "These three are built from zero. The reworks are faster, but the rebuilds need time to get right."
- Don't be defensive. Be factual. The table does the work.

---

## Slide 7 -- What Students Leave With

> "Here's what's different about the outcome. Students don't just get a certificate. They leave with four tangible things."

> "A deployed product -- live URL, shareable. Their boss can click on it Monday morning."

> "A Validation Evidence Brief -- this is the real deliverable. It says: here's the assumption we tested, here's the evidence, here's our recommendation. This is what a PM hands to their VP."

> "A Living PRD -- specs extracted from building, not written before. It shows the journey."

> "And a personal prompt library -- a reusable toolkit they can apply to any product initiative going forward."

- Hit the Validation Evidence Brief hardest. That's the one that connects to Fernan's vision of PMs who can ship. It's not about the prototype -- it's about the validated insight.

---

## Slide 8 -- How We Measure Impact

> "We defined clear metrics so we can actually tell if this is working. Four numbers."

> "NPS target: 4.5, up from 3.8. That's our headline."

> "90% build completion -- every module, 9 out of 10 students produce a working prototype. If that drops, we know the lab is broken."

> "100% live deployment -- every group ships to a real URL by the end of M6. Not localhost. A real link."

> "And the one that matters most for the business: 60% of students report using vibe coding in their actual PM work within 30 days. That's the transfer metric. That's how we know the course actually changed behavior."

- The 60% work application metric is the one Carlos should care about most. That's the business case for the course.
- If he asks "how do we measure that?" -- 30-day follow-up survey, same mechanism Product School uses for other courses.

---

## Slide 9 -- What Nobody Else Teaches

> "We looked at everyone -- Reforge, Maven, Harvard, Coursera, Zero to Mastery. Here's what we found."

> "Everyone teaches a slice. Reforge is great at problem framing but stops at prototype. Maven has hands-on courses but they're tool tutorials. Harvard has rigor but no practitioner speed."

> "We're the only ones teaching the full arc -- from 'I don't know what to build' all the way to 'here's a deployed product with validated evidence.'"

> "Our second differentiator: the graduation moment. 73% of vibe-coded apps never make it to production. The reason? Teams never deliberately transition from exploring to building for real. We teach that exact moment."

> "And third: validation as the outcome. This is a PM course, not a coding course. The build is the instrument. The validated insight is the prize."

- The 73% stat tends to land well. It makes the graduation moment feel urgent, not theoretical.
- If Carlos asks "where's that stat from?" -- it's from research on vibe-coded applications and their production success rates, and the 8x code duplication data.

## Slide 10 -- Where We Sit in the Market

> "Simple positioning. Reforge is theory-heavy, no building. Maven is broad, not AI-native. Harvard has prestige but isn't for practitioners."

> "We're practitioner-first. Build-to-learn. The complete arc from ambiguity to deployment, with validation as the outcome. Nobody else occupies that space."

- Keep this slide fast. 15-20 seconds. The visual does the work -- Product School is highlighted, everyone else is grey. Let them see it.
- If Carlos wants to dig into competitors, you have the full competitive analysis in Appendix B of the strategy doc.

## Slide 11 -- Next Steps

> "Here's where we are on timing."

> "Today we're validating the skeleton. If you're aligned on the Confidence Line framework and the module structure, we move to detailing."

> "Next week, Dana and I iterate on the framework and start building out Module 1 in detail."

> "Feb 24 I start teaching AI Agents, so my bandwidth shifts. The goal is to have the full skeleton locked and Module 1 detailed before that."

> "Target: new course ready for the next cohort."

- Be specific about what you need from Carlos right now: "Are you aligned on the Confidence Line as the framework? Are you aligned on the module structure? If yes, we start building. If you have concerns, let's talk through them now so we don't iterate on the wrong thing."
- Don't leave this slide without a clear yes/no from him.

## Slide 12 -- Why This Wins (Closing)

> "Last thing. This is not a course about AI tools. It's about how modern PMs validate intent faster than ever before. Vibe coding is the engine, not the destination."

> "Students start in ambiguity, gain confidence through building, and ship production-ready products backed by evidence. That's the pitch. That's the Confidence Line."

> "The full strategy document and instructor guide are in the repo if you want the details. But the question for today is: are we aligned on this direction?"

- End on the question. Don't end on a statement. You want Carlos to respond.
- If he says yes: "Great, we'll have Module 1 detailed by next week."
- If he says "I need to think about it": "What specifically? Let's talk through it now."
- If he says "this is too much change": point back to Slide 6 -- "we can reuse 55-60% of existing content. The rebuilds are the priority. The reworks are lighter."

---

## General Tips for the Room

- **Carlos is visual.** Don't read the slides. Let them sit. Point to things. "See this? This is the key."
- **Fernan cares about shipping.** Hit the Validation Evidence Brief and the engineering handoff. He wants PMs who can go from prototype to production.
- **Dana has your back on scope.** If timeline pressure comes up, let her speak to the effort. Slide 6 is her ammunition.
- **Don't defend the old course.** You're not saying it was bad. You're saying the packaging was wrong. The bones are good. This is an evolution, not a rejection.
- **If things get detailed,** offer the strategy doc: "It's all in the document. 600 lines of detail. But for today, I want to make sure we're aligned on the direction before we go deeper."
